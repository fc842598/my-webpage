The Health Palace, also called the Illness Palace, is not a medical diagnosis. It is a symbolic area for reading pressure, recovery habits, workload, and the parts of daily life that may need more care. Medical symptoms still belong with qualified health professionals, not with a birth chart.

Read the Palace as a Pressure Pattern

Start by asking whether the Health Palace is stable, overworked, or repeatedly activated. Then connect it to Career for workload, to the Inner Life Palace for sleep and emotional strain, and to the Travel Palace for environmental or schedule changes. A difficult Health Palace does not prove illness; it may describe a lifestyle that leaves too little room for recovery.

Stars Describe the Form, Not a Diagnosis

Mars may symbolize acute strain, heat, haste, or a tendency to ignore limits. Di Kong or Di Jie can point to depleted energy, inconsistent routines, or effort that is hard to sustain. Supportive stars may improve organization and recovery, but they do not cancel the need for ordinary care. The safest interpretation stays with observable habits and appropriate screening.

Use Transformations and Timing Carefully

For example, Hua Ji affecting the Health-Career axis may coincide with work pressure, delayed rest, or repeated worry about performance. If Hua Quan also strengthens Career, the person may carry more responsibility while having less recovery time. In an annual cycle, Mars joining an already pressured Health Palace is a prompt to slow down, follow safety procedures, and seek medical advice for actual symptoms. If the natal palace is stable and only the annual layer is tense, treat it as a temporary care period rather than a lifelong verdict.

Chart-Reading Order

First assess the natal Health Palace and its main stars. Second, read the opposite palace and the Career-Inner Life links. Third, place Hua Lu, Hua Quan, Hua Ke, and Hua Ji on the relevant axis. Fourth, compare the ten-year and annual cycles to see whether pressure is persistent or temporary. End with practical actions: sleep, workload boundaries, routine care, safety checks, and professional evaluation when needed.

Tuo Luo in Zi Wei Dou Shu: The Hidden Drag of Delay and Entanglement, and How to Break the Loop

Tuo Luo is yin metal, qi of Ji (or punishment), the most sticky of the four malefics, governing delay, repetition, entanglement, chronic problems and hidden resistance, like a self-wound cocoon. It does not erupt but slowly grinds: positively endurance and deep stamina, negatively indecision, looping and chronic issues. Break it with deadlines, small steps and external pace.

Zi Wei Dou Shu2026-09-07

Qing Yang is an open spear, Tuo Luo a hidden arrow: one is too fast and clashing, the other too slow and sticky, dragging matters into repeated loops; the fix is not more force but winding a spring for action.

What This Question Is Really Asking

Tuo Luo people feel they overthink and cannot start, mistaking drag for laziness; willpower alone fails the sticky nature. Deadlines, checklists and external commitment shrinking the first step are what break it.

The Underlying Logic

Tuo Luo is yin metal, qi of Ji, always one palace after Lu Cun, governing delay, entanglement, repetition and chronic drain—a hidden arrow with indirect, lasting effect.

Positive: strong endurance and depth, outlasting long processes, suited to crafts and research needing repeated polishing.

Negative: indecisive, dragging, prone to hidden obstacles and chronic issues (including health concerns, defer to medical care).

Break it: hard deadlines, splitting tasks into immediately doable steps, external pace (partners, check-ins, appointments) to force action, avoiding perfectionist stall.

A Step-by-Step Way to Read It

  1. Locate Tuo Luo to see which matters drag and stick most
  2. Accept sticky nature over laziness; stop blame, use mechanisms
  3. Set external hard deadlines with no open-endedness
  4. Shrink the first step to ten minutes; finish before perfecting
  5. Use partners, appointments and check-ins for external pace
  6. Seek timely care for chronic concerns; never let metaphysics delay treatment

Three Common Mistakes

Mistake 1: Taking delay as laziness and relying on willpower

Mistake 2: Pursuing perfection and never delivering

Mistake 3: Dragging feet even on chronic health symptoms

Back to Your Own Chart

Tuo Luo's lesson is to move. It wears you not by striking but by self-entanglement; wind action with deadlines and small steps, and slow stamina becomes depth rather than drain.

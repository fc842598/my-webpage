Qi Sha, Po Jun, and Tan Lang in the Spouse Palace: How to Arrange Family Around a Changing Rhythm

How the change-oriented stars call for early communication, buffer time, and stable anchors such as savings and fixed family time.

Zi Wei Dou Shu2026-11-17 10:42

The group of Qi Sha, Po Jun, and Tan Lang all carry change, creation, and an unwillingness to stay still. In the Spouse Palace they often indicate many rhythm changes in the relationship, such as work changes, moves, career shifts, or one side often having new ideas. This rhythm is not bad in itself, but the family must be arranged well so repeated changes do not consume the relationship.

The changing nature of this star group

Qi Sha governs tackling and decisions, Po Jun governs breaking and starting over, and Tan Lang governs change and new desires, all leaning toward movement. In the Spouse Palace, the relationship easily goes through stage-by-stage adjustments rather than a straight line. Admitting this rhythm first lets changes be included in family arrangements rather than handled on the spot each time.

Step-by-step rhythm arrangement check

  1. Confirm which star is in the Spouse Palace, Qi Sha, Po Jun, or Tan Lang, and understand its changing nature.
  2. Discuss foreseeable changes in advance, such as work, moves, or shifts.
  3. Agree to communicate before major changes and leave the family preparation and buffer time.
  4. Combine the transformations to see whether the change brings resources or blockages, then judge the rhythm.
  5. Write the family arrangements after the change, costs, time, and housing, so the family does not receive it passively.

Keep a stable anchor within change

However fond of change, the family should keep some fixed things, such as stable savings and fixed time together, as anchors within change. Arranging the rhythm well keeps the creating drive from becoming relationship unease.

Chart reading order

  1. Fix the chart and confirm the Spouse Palace stars and transformations.
  2. Understand the changing nature first, then list foreseeable changes.
  3. Communicate major changes first and leave buffer time.
  4. Use savings and fixed time as stable anchors.
  5. Keep the conclusion conditional rather than treating change as an accident.

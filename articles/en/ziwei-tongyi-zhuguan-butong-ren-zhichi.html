Zi Wei Dou Shu: Observing One Manager's Different Support for Different People

Do not label a manager from first impressions; ask observable questions about who gets what support in which situation.

Zi Wei Dou Shu2026-11-06 11:35

On one team, some people find the manager difficult while others find her protective and willing to give chances. Why do opinions of the same person differ so much? What kind of person a manager is cannot be judged from a label or first impression. The reliable approach is to break it into observable behavior and then use real contact to correct assumptions.

Assumptions get corrected by real contact

After one conflict or a harsh word, it is easy to label a manager as difficult or biased. But a single star or first impression only shows a tendency and cannot replace long-term observation. The same person may behave quite differently in different situations, and early judgments often need updating. Set aside “I feel she is targeting me” and return to what actually happened.

Turn vague labels into observable questions

Instead of asking whether the manager dislikes me, ask observable questions: to whom, in which setting, and in what form is support given? Is it public criticism, a private reminder, or resources and room to try? Do expectations differ for newcomers and senior staff? Record how she treats me alongside how she treats others, and see whether the difference is a stable pattern or only appears in specific situations.

Case and method

A newcomer felt the manager was harsh for questioning details in public and considered leaving. After a few months, she saw the same standard applied to everyone, while the manager actively sought promotions for reliable senior staff, and the newcomer eventually received the same chances. Another subordinate found the manager's support was only verbal and resources were not actually provided, which was the real difference to watch for. Read the Career Palace for work style and the Friends Palace for team relations, then confirm with observable behavior rather than letting the palaces label a person. Read the tendency, list concrete behavioral questions, and verify through real contact over time so observation, not labels, decides the next step.

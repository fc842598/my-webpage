If the Floating Rate Rises, Can You Still Cover the Mortgage?

Why today's low rate is not enough, and how to run a step-by-step interest-rate stress test to find the risk line.

Zi Wei Dou Shu2026-11-17 09:06

Many mortgages use a floating rate, so an easy payment today does not mean an easy payment later. When rates rise, the same principal brings a higher monthly payment and slowly consumes the margin that was just enough. The Property Palace is about stable living, and real stability must survive a rate stress test rather than relying on today's rate.

Why the current rate alone is not enough

The current rate is one moment, while repayment lasts many years. Calculating only at the lowest rate assumes the future stays loose. When rates rise, the payment difference has to come from the living margin. Working out the higher-rate figures too shows whether the home is affordable in different conditions.

Step-by-step rate stress test

  1. Note the payment at today's rate and the living margin left after it.
  2. Use a calculator to assume the rate rises by a few points, then find each payment.
  3. Compare the payment difference before and after, checking whether the margin absorbs it.
  4. If the margin turns negative at a certain level, treat that level as the clear risk line.
  5. If the risk line is too tight, lower the price, raise the down payment, or consider a fixed plan.

The Property Palace and the risk line

If the Property Palace leans steady, it values long-term affordability and suits a stress test that finds the risk line early. If the stars lean impulsive, beware of acting only on today's low rate. The chart gives a tendency, while the real higher-rate figures decide whether it is affordable.

Chart reading order

  1. Fix the chart and Property Palace, then confirm your attitude toward risk.
  2. Calculate the payment and living margin at the current rate.
  3. Raise the rate step by step to find where the margin turns negative.
  4. Use the risk line to decide the price, down payment, and rate plan.
  5. Keep the conclusion conditional rather than treating today's low rate as permanent.

When the Four Transformations Clash in Zi Wei Dou Shu: Quan-Ji Battle, Ke-Ji Tangle and Lu-Ji Opposition

When one star or palace carries two transformations, tension forms: Quan meeting Ji is the tug of wanting control yet being blocked, Ke meeting Ji is the tangle of wanting grace amid recurring trouble, Lu meeting Ji is simultaneous gain and loss. They do not cancel; read which dominates and in which stage.

Zi Wei Dou Shu2026-09-10

Many assume Lu and Ji in one palace cancel out; in reality two things happen at once—money in while money leaks, or sweet first and bitter later—which never truly cancels.

What This Question Is Really Asking

Asking why a job gives you power yet makes you suffer, or why a relationship splits and reconciles without a clean break, you are usually watching two transformations battle in the same place.

The Underlying Logic

Quan-Ji battle: Quan pushes to control while Ji tightens the knot; together or opposed it shows "the harder you grip, the more it sticks, and the more it sticks the harder you grip"—authority paired with obstruction at work, control paired with resentment in love. The fix is loosening the Ji knot, not more force.

Ke-Ji tangle: Ke wants smooth grace while Ji keeps creating problems, appearing as a polished surface with constant patching underneath—saving face, repeated rework, reputation tangled with dispute; it tends to drag, and Ke's order must digest Ji step by step.

Lu-Ji opposition (same or opposite palace): Lu gives and Ji closes, often gain and loss within one matter. Lu visible and Ji hidden means gain then loss; Ji visible and Lu hidden means hardship then gain—read by stage, not a single verdict.

Never add/subtract the two: first use brightness to see which is stronger, then whether a third transformation (e.g., Lu-Quan-Ji in trine, or Ke to the rescue) intervenes, then use decade/year to see which dominates in each stage. A clash reads as a process tug, not a static result.

A Step-by-Step Way to Read It

  1. Find the two transformations sharing one star, one palace or opposite palaces
  2. Identify Quan-Ji, Ke-Ji or Lu-Ji to set the type of tug
  3. Do not cancel; write the real-life expression of each separately
  4. Use star brightness and any third transformation that rescues to see which dominates
  5. Stage it by decade/year: when transformation A leads and when B takes over
  6. Address the dominant, Ji-side first rather than spreading effort evenly

Three Common Mistakes

Mistake 1: Treating two transformations as numbers that cancel, missing that two matters run in parallel

Mistake 2: Labeling "Quan-Ji battle" without explaining the concrete tug in career or love

Mistake 3: Ignoring brightness and a third transformation, reaching a static good/bad verdict

Back to Your Own Chart

A transformation clash is not about winning but how a person is pulled between two forces and which knot to loosen at which stage. Understanding the distinct Quan-Ji, Ke-Ji and Lu-Ji scripts gives a far finer read on the things that never come easy.

Reading Order

  1. Cast the natal chart first and locate the palaces and major stars tied to pairwise clashes among the four transformations before judging anything
  2. Open the triple-direction of those palaces and count the auspicious, malefic and assistant stars separately
  3. Check where the natal Four Transformations land to set the chart's baseline tone
  4. Read the decade-limit transformations to see whether the pairwise clashes among the four transformations line is activated in this stage
  5. Then read the annual transformations and the year's palace to pin down this year's trigger and timing
  6. Layer natal, decade and year before concluding, then decide how to act rather than labeling good or bad
